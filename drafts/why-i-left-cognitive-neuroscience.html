Why I left cognitive neuroscience

Date 28 January 2026 Modified 13 March 2026 By Johan Carlin Tags academia / personal / neuroscience

Leaving academia is a big deal. It's giving up on a lifestyle, a community, a cult of sorts. Yet most of us leave quietly, quickly losing touch with the literature and our colleagues. I found that I actively avoided mentions of my old field in the news, as though it were an ex partner.

I always wanted to write something about why I left. Close to 5 years on I feel like I have enough distance to get it done. At the same time, the following reads a bit raw. There's a reason for that. It's actually adapted from a voice memo I recorded while driving just months after leaving, when I was very much still coming to terms with what had happened. I feel more detached these days, but I think the core views here remain my own. So here goes.

Publish or perish, or just fading away

While many departures are framed as sudden choices, the reality is often simpler: you run out of runway. If you have not published enough papers to remain competitive for a permanent position, your days are numbered. Leaving is rarely a discrete moment; it is a gradual process of stopping the game, ceasing the chase for papers, and perhaps becoming an academic dissident for a while. If you have the luxury of doing this, at least. I ended my career on a permanent contract, a sort of post-doc for life.

The incentive to game the system

The fundamental issue I couldn't overcome lies in academia’s incentive structures. Grant bodies and hiring committees reward apparent novelty and high productivity above all else. In such a hyper-competitive environment, survival is nearly impossible if you do not conform and start cranking out papers reporting seemingly novel results.

Systemic reform based on a moral awakening is unlikely to succeed. As long as the incentives remain unchanged, those with a mercenary attitude will outcompete those attempting to do the right thing. This creates a selection pressure for scientists willing to game the system. Eventually, you realise that even well-intended improvements like requiring power analyses simply become another hurdle for scientists to clear without actually changing their underlying behaviour. Scientists are adept at meeting any quantitative target you set them without doing things differently.

MRI scanners as expensive artifact factories

In cognitive neuroscience, the situation is particularly fraught because our signal is indirect. We measure a slow vascular response that is only loosely linked to neural activity, effectively smoothing over space and time. Getting this right requires tremendous care, but care is rarely a recipe for a productive academic career.

I came to believe that when you see something exciting in your neuroimaging data, your prior should be that it is an artifact stemming from head motion, experimental design, or pre-processing. That's not to say that there is no signal, but you have to always contend with the fact that the noise sources are stronger so as soon as they covary with the signal you end up in trouble. However, you cannot build a career on papers that conclude you thought you found something, but actually just failed to correct for head motion properly.

To make this more concrete. Towards the end of my career I was loosely involved with a project that attempted to combine layer-resolution functional MRI with a between subjects design investigating a psychiatric sample compared to controls. Concretely, this is taking a technique that is at the very edge of what is physically possible to measure, even in a higher powered within-subjects design using experienced participants. And combining that with a small psychiatric sample that is far less likely to start tremendously still during the imaging. The group attempting this had no particular expertise in running this kind of advanced neuroimaging study. Under these conditions it doesn't matter too much whether the hypothesis being tested holds or not. The chance of making a genuine discovery is practically zero, but there will be a lot of interesting-looking noise, and the ambitious nature of the study was sure to guarantee interest in high impact journals regardless of the truth value of the findings. You come across enough cases like that and the whole thing starts you seem a house of cards.

A field built on shaky foundations

There is also a concerning cultural bias within the field. Many researchers come from cognitive backgrounds and lack strong quantitative foundations in physics or engineering. We see a tradition where principal investigators, often trained in cognitive psychology, spin yarns about blobs provided by post-docs, despite having missing foundational knowledge regarding the MRI signal itself.

This leads to a terrifying situation where new methods for studying "connectivity” are likely just describing new artifacts. If you read the literature, it seems functional MRI can decode almost anything, from your position in a social network to a mental number line. Yet, when you look closely, these findings are often inconsistent and lack specificity, driven by low statistical power rather than genuine discovery.

The black pill conclusion

Hanging on as a disillusioned academic, as I did towards the end, can feel like being part of a black pill community, where you and your friends grumble together about the latest nonsense published in high-impact journals. This is a decent coping strategy but over the long term it seems ultimately detrimental to your mental health.

Ultimately, I left because I became cynical about the possibility of change. The prospect of spending a career begging people to do slightly better research felt increasingly bleak. In a system that rewards the simple story over the messy truth, sometimes the only winning move is to stop playing.
